3-player and 2-player Mahjong house rules

American Mahjong is designed for four, but life doesn’t always cooperate. Someone cancels, a friend wants to practice, or you’re teaching one person at the kitchen table. Groups have found ways to play short-handed. There’s no single standard, so treat everything below as common house rules to agree on before you start.

What stays the same

Most of the game doesn’t change with fewer players:

  • You still win by building exactly 14 tiles that match one line of the card.
  • The joker rules are identical: jokers only in groups of three or more, never in singles or pairs ⚠ check with your group.
  • Calling discards works the same way: only to finish an exposed group of three or more, or to declare Mahjong.
  • Scoring uses the same values and the same doubling for jokerless hands.

The main things that change are the Charleston, what happens to the empty seat, and who pays after a win.

Three players

The Charleston

With no one across the table, the across pass has nowhere to go. Many groups simply skip it: pass right, then left ⚠ check with your group. Some groups still do a second Charleston the same way; others skip it to keep the game moving. If you play a courtesy pass, decide who it’s with, since there’s no “across”.

The empty seat

The fourth player’s section of the wall is the biggest question. Some groups still build all four walls and play from them as usual; others leave the empty seat’s wall out ⚠ check with your group. Building all four keeps the number of tiles in play the same as a normal game. Leaving one out makes games shorter, with fewer chances to draw the tile you need. Either works, as long as everyone knows which you’re using.

Scoring

With three players, payments follow the usual rules with two payers instead of three ⚠ check with your group. Off a discard, the discarder pays twice the value and the other player pays once. On a self-pick, both other players pay twice.

Two players

The Charleston

With just two, you sit across from each other, so many groups play the Charleston with across passes only ⚠ check with your group. Agree on how many passes you’ll do; a short Charleston keeps the game brisk.

Tiles in play

Some groups take certain tiles out of the game when only two are playing ⚠ check with your group, to make hands come together at a reasonable pace. Which tiles, if any, varies widely, so decide together and write it down for next time.

Scoring

With two players, a discard win is usually paid by the discarder only, and a self-pick by the one other player ⚠ check with your group. Some pairs skip points altogether and just play for practice.

Questions to settle before you start

  1. Which Charleston passes will you do, and will there be a second Charleston?
  2. Is there a courtesy pass, and with whom?
  3. Will you build all four walls, or leave the empty seat’s wall out?
  4. For two players: will any tiles be taken out of the game?
  5. How will payments work, and do any penalties apply?
  6. How does the dealer role move with fewer seats?

Write your answers on a sticky note and keep it with your set. Next time you’re short a player, you won’t have to negotiate from scratch.

Tips for short-handed games

  • Expect different strategy. With fewer players, fewer discards come your way, so concealed hands can become more realistic.
  • Use it for teaching. A two-player game is a relaxed way to explain the card, the Charleston and jokers without three people waiting.
  • Keep the same card. Short-handed games still use the regular yearly card, so practice carries over to your full table.
  • Agree on time. Short games are quicker; decide how many rounds you’ll play.

A sample three-player evening

Here’s how one group might run it. Before starting, they agree: build all four walls, pass right then left in the first Charleston, no second Charleston, no courtesy pass, and the usual payments with two payers. The dealer still starts with 14 tiles and passes the role to the right after every hand ⚠ check with your group, skipping the empty seat. Play is otherwise exactly like a four-player game, and the night ends after a set number of rounds. Your group may choose differently, and that’s fine: the point is that everyone agrees before the first tile is thrown.

Why there’s no single answer

American Mahjong rules are written for four players, and short-handed play grew up group by group. That’s why two experienced players can describe it differently and both be right for their table. When you join a new group, ask how they handle it. It’s a friendly question, and every group has an answer.
