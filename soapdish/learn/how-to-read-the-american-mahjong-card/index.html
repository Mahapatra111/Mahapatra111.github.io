How to read the American Mahjong card

The card is the heart of American Mahjong. Once you can read one line, you can read them all. This guide walks you through it slowly, using examples from our own practice card rather than any publisher’s card.

What the card is

American Mahjong is played toward a list of winning hands printed on a card. A new card comes out every year, and everyone at the table plays from the same one. You win by building exactly 14 tiles that match one line of the card. That’s the whole goal: pick a line, collect its tiles, and be the first to finish.

Unlike many other mahjong styles, you don’t invent your own combinations. If it isn’t on the card, it isn’t a winning hand. That makes the card both your rulebook and your menu.

Sections: hands that share an idea

The card is split into sections, each built around one idea. Common ideas include a year written in tiles, even numbers, odd numbers, numbers in a row, the same number in different suits, winds and dragons, and hands made only of singles and pairs. Reading the section heading first tells you what the hands below it have in common, which makes the lines much easier to understand.

A line is a pattern, not a fixed hand

Each line describes a pattern that can be built in many ways. Here’s a line from our Starter Card:

FF 22 444 666 8888 · Any 2 suits · 25 points · X

Read it one group at a time. FF means two flowers. 22 is a pair of 2s, 444 three 4s, 666 three 6s and 8888 four 8s. Together that’s 2 + 2 + 3 + 3 + 4 = 14 tiles. The note “any 2 suits” tells you that the numbers come from two different suits, which brings us to the most important skill: reading the colors.

Colors show suit relationships

On the printed card, numbers appear in different ink colors. The colors don’t stand for particular suits. Instead, groups printed in the same color must be the same suit, and groups in different colors must be different suits. In our example, if the 2s and 4s are printed in one color and the 6s and 8s in another, you could build 2s and 4s in Bams with 6s and 8s in Dots, or 2s and 4s in Craks with 6s and 8s in Bams, and so on. Any suit can play any color, as long as same-color groups match and different-color groups don’t.

The three suits are Bams, Craks and Dots, each numbered 1 to 9. Each suit also has its own dragon: Red goes with Craks, Green with Bams, and White (often called Soap) with Dots. When a dragon on the card is printed in the same color as a suit group, it means that suit’s own dragon ⚠ check with your group.

Words you’ll see on lines

Short notes beside a line narrow down how it can be built. The most common are:

  • Like numbers: the same number in different suits; consecutive run: numbers in a row, starting anywhere they fit; any 1, 2 or 3 suits: how many suits the numbers use; matching dragon: the dragon that goes with a suit; opposite dragon: a dragon that doesn’t.
  • Zero: in hands that spell a year, the White Dragon (Soap) stands for 0 and has no suit.

Here’s a second Starter Card line that uses a run and a matching dragon:

11 222 3333 444 DD · Any 4 numbers in a row, 1 suit, pair of its matching dragon · 25 points · X

“11 222 3333 444” is shown with 1, 2, 3 and 4 as an example, but any four numbers in a row work: 3 4 5 6 or 6 7 8 9 would also do. All of them come from one suit, and the pair of dragons must be that suit’s dragon. So 5s through 8s in Craks would need two Red Dragons.

Group sizes have names

You’ll hear players name groups by size: a single (1 tile), a pair (2), a pung (3), a kong (4), a quint (5) and a sextet (6). A group is always made of identical tiles, and all flowers count as identical. Because only four copies of each number, wind and dragon exist, a quint or sextet of them always needs jokers, while flowers can make one on their own.

Points, X and C

At the end of each line you’ll find two more pieces of information. The number is the line’s point value, and the X or C tells you whether the hand may be exposed. An X line lets you call discards to make groups that you place face-up on the table. A C line must stay concealed on your rack the whole time; only the very last tile, the one that completes Mahjong, may come from a discard. Concealed hands are harder, so they usually carry more points.

Points matter after a win: they decide how many points each player pays the winner. Our guide to scoring at game night explains the details.

How to choose the hands you aim for

New players often try to keep every line in mind and get overwhelmed. Here’s a calmer approach:

  1. Look at your tiles by suit and number. Do you have lots of even numbers? Several of one number in different suits? Plenty of flowers or winds?
  2. Find two or three lines that fit. Lines that share tiles with each other are ideal, because you can keep your options open through the Charleston.
  3. Prefer flexible lines early. A line that allows any suits or any run gives you more ways to finish than one that names exact tiles.
  4. Commit before you expose. Once you place a group face-up, it has to fit the line you’re making, so choose your line before calling tiles.
  5. Watch what others expose. Their groups hint at the lines they’re making, and at the tiles they might want from you.

Reading the card gets faster

For your first few games, keep the card in your hand and read lines out loud as you go. After a handful of game nights, the patterns start to jump out: you’ll glance at your rack and spot two promising lines in seconds. A helper like SoapDish can speed up that learning by ranking every line by how many tiles you still need, so you can see why one line is closer than another.

Every year brings a new card with new lines, so even experienced players start each spring by reading it slowly. You’re in good company.
