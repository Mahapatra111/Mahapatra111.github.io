The Charleston explained step by step

Before the first tile is discarded, American Mahjong players trade tiles in a ritual called the Charleston. It’s the best chance you’ll get to shape your hand, and it’s much simpler than it first looks.

Why the Charleston exists

Everyone starts with a random set of tiles. The Charleston lets each player pass along tiles that don’t help them and receive tiles that might. Done well, it turns a messy starting rack into a hand with a clear direction. Done carelessly, it can send you away from the hand you had. The good news: the steps are always the same.

Before you start

The dealer, called East, starts with 14 tiles and everyone else with 13. Take a moment to sort your rack by suit and number and glance at the card. You don’t need to choose your final hand yet. You only need a rough idea of which tiles are useful and which are strays.

The first Charleston: right, across, left

The first Charleston is played by everyone: pass 3 tiles to the right, then 3 across, then 3 to the left. For every pass, choose and pass your 3 tiles before you look at the tiles coming to you ⚠ check with your group. That keeps the pass fair and keeps the game moving.

  1. Right. Pick the three tiles that help you least and pass them face-down to the player on your right. Then pick up the three tiles passed to you.
  2. Across. Re-sort, choose three more tiles to give away and pass them to the player across the table.
  3. Left. Same again, to the player on your left. On this last pass you have an extra option, described next.

Passing on tiles unseen (the blind pass)

On the last pass of each Charleston, you may pass on 1, 2 or 3 of the tiles coming to you without looking at them, and make up the rest of your 3 from your own rack ⚠ check with your group. Players do this when they’ve found three tiles they don’t want to break up: instead of giving away a useful tile, they send the incoming tiles straight on. It’s often called “stealing” or a blind pass.

The second Charleston (optional)

After the first Charleston, the table can choose to go again. The second Charleston happens only if every player agrees; it goes left, then across, then right, and any one player can choose to stop it ⚠ check with your group. If you like your hand already, you can say no and the second Charleston is skipped for everyone. If it goes ahead, the last pass (right) again allows a blind pass.

The courtesy pass

To finish, you and the player across from you may make one more swap. In the courtesy pass, you each offer 0, 1, 2 or 3 tiles; if you ask for different numbers, you swap the smaller number ⚠ check with your group. Some groups skip it entirely; it’s always optional.

Through all of this, the dealer still has 14 tiles and everyone else 13. Passing moves tiles around; it never changes how many you hold.

Never pass a joker

One rule has no exceptions: jokers are never passed, in the Charleston or the courtesy pass. Jokers are the most flexible tiles in the game, so you’d want to keep them anyway, but even if you didn’t, passing them isn’t allowed.

Which tiles to pass

There’s no single right answer, but these habits help most beginners:

  • Keep pairs and pungs. Two or three of the same tile are hard to collect later. Hold on to them unless they truly don’t fit any line.
  • Keep flowers if lines want them. Many hands use flowers, and there are only eight in the game.
  • Pass the orphans. A lone tile that fits none of the lines you’re considering is the first to go.
  • Stay flexible early. On the first pass, keep tiles that fit two or three possible lines. By the last pass, start narrowing down.
  • Watch what comes back. The tiles you receive hint at what your neighbors are throwing away, and so what they’re not collecting.
  • Don’t feed a neighbor. If you notice the player on your right keeps passing you dragons, they probably aren’t collecting them. What you pass them, though, may help their hand, so avoid sending a tile you think they want.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Looking before passing. Pick your three tiles first, then look at what arrived.
  • Breaking up a pair too early. It feels safe to pass a tile you have two of, but pairs are valuable.
  • Chasing one exact line from the first pass. Starting narrow makes the Charleston work against you.
  • Forgetting the blind-pass option. On the last pass of each Charleston it can save a tile you’d otherwise have to give up.

A tool to practice with

If you’d like a second opinion while you learn, SoapDish’s Charleston Coach looks at your rack on each pass, suggests three tiles to send, and explains which lines it’s keeping open.
