How to score and settle up at game night

When someone calls Mahjong, the table pauses to check the hand and work out who pays the winner how many points. It sounds fiddly, but there are only a few cases, and once you’ve done it a couple of times it takes seconds.

Where points come from

Every line on the card has a point value printed beside it. That number is the starting value of the winning hand. Then two questions decide the payments: how did the player win, and did they use any jokers?

Step 1: check the hand

When a player declares Mahjong, their 14 tiles (rack plus exposed groups) must exactly match one line of the card, and the other players check the hand before paying. Lay the tiles out clearly, find the line on the card, and confirm colors, numbers and any joker use. If something’s off, our guide to mahjong terms explains what happens with a Mahjong called by mistake.

Step 2: how did they win?

There are two ways to win, and they pay differently.

Won off a discard

If the winning tile was someone else’s discard, the player who discarded it pays twice the hand’s value, and each of the other two players pays the value once ⚠ check with your group.

Self-pick

If the winner drew the winning tile from the wall themselves, all three other players pay twice the value ⚠ check with your group.

Step 3: jokerless doubles the value

If the winning hand uses no jokers at all, the hand’s value doubles before the payments above are worked out. The exception: hands that can’t use jokers anyway, such as hands made only of singles and pairs, don’t get the bonus ⚠ check with your group.

Worked examples

Let’s use four players: Ann, Bea, Cat and Dee.

  1. 25-point hand, won off Bea’s discard. Bea pays 50 (twice the value). Cat pays 25 and Dee pays 25. Ann collects 100 in total.
  2. 25-point hand, self-picked by Ann. Bea, Cat and Dee each pay 50. Ann collects 150.
  3. 25-point jokerless hand, self-picked. The value doubles to 50, then everyone pays twice that: 100 each. Ann collects 300.
  4. 50-point singles-and-pairs hand, won off a discard. No jokerless bonus, because the hand can’t use jokers anyway. The discarder pays 100; the other two pay 50 each.

Notice how much a self-pick and a jokerless hand add up together. That’s why players sometimes hold out for a real tile rather than using a joker.

When nobody wins

If the wall runs out before anyone completes a hand, the hand ends with no winner, called a wall game, and nobody pays. Some groups add a small wall-game payment as a house rule; agree on it before you start.

Three players

With three at the table, the same rules apply with two payers instead of three ⚠ check with your group. Off a discard, the discarder pays twice and the other player pays once; on a self-pick, both pay twice. See 3-player and 2-player house rules for more.

House rules worth agreeing on

Groups vary, so settle these before the first hand:

  • Penalties for mistakes. Custom penalties, such as for a Mahjong called in error, are a house rule.
  • Throwing the winning tile to a big exposure. Some groups make the discarder pay for everyone if they throw the winning tile to a player with several groups showing ⚠ check with your group.
  • Wall-game payments, as above.

Keeping score over a whole night

Most groups keep a running tally rather than settling every hand. A simple way:

  1. Write each player’s name across the top of a page.
  2. After each hand, add the winner’s points and subtract each payer’s points.
  3. Check the row adds up to zero: what the winner gains, the others lose.
  4. Record wall games too, so you know when the dealer moved on.
  5. At the end of the night, the totals show who’s ahead.

Some groups show their totals in cents for fun. Whatever your group does, it’s simply a way of keeping score.

Don’t forget the dealer

After every hand, win or not, the dealer role moves to the next player ⚠ check with your group, going to the right (counter-clockwise) ⚠ check with your group. Keeping score on paper is a good place to note who deals next.

A quick checklist after every Mahjong

  1. Did the hand match one line exactly, with 14 tiles?
  2. Were jokers used only in groups of three or more?
  3. Was the winning tile a discard (who threw it?) or a self-pick?
  4. Any jokers at all? If not, and the hand could have used them, double the value.
  5. Write down the payments, then pass the dealer role on.

Running through these five questions out loud also helps newer players learn the scoring without slowing the game down.
